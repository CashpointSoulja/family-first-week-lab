# Decision log

First-person record of the product calls I made for this concept. None of these were validated with users; each says what would change my mind.

| # | Decision | Why | Alternatives I rejected | What would change my mind |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | I put consent as step 2, before any setup or data, and made it impossible for the family member to accept. | The monitored person is the one with rights, and a product that feels like being watched risks refusal or quiet unplugging. A hard gate in the engine is safer than a UI convention. | Family accepts and the person is "notified"; setup first, consent later. | Legal or safeguarding advice that a supported-decision route is needed (that would be a separate flow, never a bypass). |
| D2 | I made sharing per category with nothing pre-ticked. | Pre-ticked boxes aren't meaningful consent. Bathroom and night movement feel more sensitive than kettle use, so people should choose. | "Share everything" default with opt-out. | Discovery showing per-category choice confuses people; then I'd test grouped presets, still unticked. |
| D3 | I treated missing and stale data as "unknown", never as reassurance. | False reassurance is the worst failure for a family product. A hub that has been offline for 30 hours must not produce a calm summary. | Hiding missing sensors; showing the last known state. | Nothing. This is a safety floor, though the copy itself could change. |
| D4 | I held the first insight back until 24 h of data and ≥60% of shared sensors reporting. | One evening of data says nothing about routine. Showing "too early" honestly beats a hollow card. | Instant insight from the first events. | Evidence that families drop off in the first 24 h, in which case I'd show setup-progress value rather than lower the bar. |
| D5 | I wrote observations ("kettle first used around 07:40") rather than interpretations. | Sensors show device use, not who or why. Interpretation drifts toward diagnosis, which the product can't support. | Wellbeing scores; red/amber/green health status. | Clinical validation of a specific measure, which is out of scope here. |
| D6 | I compare days 1–4 with days 5–7 for week-1 change, with an absolute and a relative threshold. | It's simple, explainable and avoids flagging 1-vs-2 events. Week 1 has no long baseline. | Standard-deviation bands (too few points); ML anomaly detection (unexplainable, needs data). | Real distributions from a pilot, which would let me set thresholds per category. |
| D7 | I made a weekly digest the default and left out real-time alerts. | Lilli publicly says it doesn't currently detect falls, so a real-time ping could be read as an emergency service. Calm defaults protect trust. | Daily default; instant "unusual activity" pushes. | Discovery showing families want daily notes, tested in Experiment 2. |
| D8 | I suggest a relaxed check-in ("How's your week been?") as the next step on change. | It protects the relationship. Asking "why did you open the door less?" turns the data into an interrogation. | "Call now" urgency; showing raw event logs. | Monitored people saying they prefer directness. |
| D9 | I show the monitored person which notification option the family chose. | Transparency both ways builds trust. | Hidden settings. | Monitored people finding it noisy. |
| D10 | I put every number on the Lab page behind a "synthetic" label and didn't compare variants. | Without real data, showing a lift would be invented. | A pretty A/B chart. | Real experiment data. |
| D11 | I used Lilli's real logo, colours, fonts and app motifs, with a constant "independent concept · not Lilli" badge. | The brief asks for a recognisably Lilli feel. The badge and footer prevent any confusion about affiliation. | A generic brand. | A request from the brand owner. |
